The big idea

After the religious wars ended with the Peace of Westphalia (1648), European states centralized power, and political and religious authority merged under the state. Countries went one of two ways:

Absolutism

Monarchs consolidate all state power under themselves to advance the needs of their state (France, Russia, Prussia, Austria).

Constitutionalism

The monarch's power is limited by law and a representative body (England after 1688, the Dutch Republic).

1. The rise of absolutist states

Why absolutism took root

  • The Catholic Church's influence weakened: growing acceptance and tolerance of Protestant practice, and little appetite for religious warfare after Westphalia.
  • Merchant classes expanded rapidly during the era of global trade. They wanted the economic and political stability absolute monarchs could provide, and power shifted away from the landed nobility toward the merchants.

Divine right of kings: a monarch's authority comes directly from God, so they answer to no earthly body.

2. France under Louis XIV
  • Cardinal Mazarin ran the government while Louis was a child. The noble revolts of the Fronde convinced Louis never to let the nobility threaten him again.
  • Intendant system: royal officials sent to the provinces to collect taxes and enforce the king's will, undermining local governors and nobles.
  • Palace of Versailles: nobles were required to live at court, kept busy with ritual and away from their regional power bases.
  • Revocation of the Edict of Nantes (1685): ended toleration for Huguenots (French Protestants). Many skilled Huguenots fled France.
  • Jean-Baptiste Colbert, finance minister, used mercantilist policies to decrease debt, revive domestic industries, expand colonies and create a favorable balance of trade.
3. Russia under Peter the Great

Peter wanted to Westernize Russia and make it a European great power.

Political reform

  • Required nobles to serve in the army or the civil administration.
  • Created the Table of Ranks: promotion by merit, so experts ended up on top.

Religious reform

  • Eliminated the role of Patriarch of the Russian Orthodox Church.
  • Replaced it with the Holy Synod, officials who did Peter's bidding, putting the church under the state.
4. England: the road to constitutionalism
  • James I and Charles I (Stuarts) believed in divine right and clashed with Parliament over taxes and religion.
  • Puritans in Parliament opposed Charles's religious policies and his attempts to rule without Parliament.
  • English Civil War (1642–1649): Parliament beat the royalists; Charles I was executed in 1649.
  • Oliver Cromwell ruled as Lord Protector; his rule became an unpopular military dictatorship.
  • Restoration (1660): monarchy restored under Charles II.
  • Glorious Revolution (1688): James II pushed out and replaced by William and Mary without major bloodshed. The English Bill of Rights (1689) limited the monarch and secured Parliament's power: a constitutional monarchy.
5. The Dutch Republic
  • Grew out of the Protestant revolt against Catholic Spain (including iconoclastic attacks on Catholic churches like Antwerp's in 1566).
  • Became one of Europe's wealthiest commercial empires in its Golden Age.
  • Governed as an oligarchy: a republic run by wealthy merchant elites, not a king.
  • Known for relative religious toleration and for trade and finance: the Dutch East India Company and the Amsterdam banking system.
6. Balance of power

Key idea: after Westphalia, the main reason for wars was to maintain the balance of power so no single state could dominate.

  • Dutch War (1672–1678): Louis XIV attacked the Dutch Republic; other states joined to check France.
  • Partitions of Poland (1772–1795): Poland-Lithuania had a weak elected monarchy and no strong central state, so Russia, Prussia and Austria carved it up until it vanished. The cost of failing to centralize.
  • War of the Spanish Succession (1701–1714): Louis XIV's grandson Philip V inherited Spain, raising fears of a France-Spain superpower.
    • Grand Alliance: England, the Dutch, Austria, Prussia vs. France, Spain, Bavaria.
    • Treaty of Utrecht (1713): Philip kept Spain, but the French and Spanish crowns could never be united.
  • Military revolution: large standing armies, new tactics and fortifications. States that expanded their militaries ended up on the winning side.
7. The Agricultural Revolution
  • The old three-field system left a third of the land unused each year.
  • New methods: crop rotation (clover, turnips), the seed drill, the improved cast-iron plow.
  • Enclosure of common lands consolidated farms and pushed many peasants toward wage work.
  • Columbian Exchange crops, especially the potato, increased calories and drove population growth.
8. Economic developments
  • Cottage industry: goods made mainly in people's homes before factories.
  • Putting-out system: merchants supplied raw materials to rural families, who made finished goods at home and were paid per piece.
  • Banking: banks kept only part of deposits and loaned the rest as venture capital, repaid with interest.
  • Insurance: for a premium, insurers covered catastrophic losses, making risky ventures like overseas trade more attractive.
  • Mercantilism: a nation grows strong by increasing its gold and silver and running a favorable balance of trade. Colonies supplied raw materials and bought finished goods.
  • Colonial empires: Britain, France, Portugal, Spain and the Netherlands competed for overseas territory.
9. The Atlantic slave trade & consumer culture
  • Atlantic slave trade: millions of enslaved Africans were forcibly transported to the Americas to work plantations.
  • Middle Passage: the brutal Atlantic crossing, with very high death rates.
  • Triangular trade: manufactured goods Europe → Africa; enslaved people Africa → Americas; cash crops (sugar, tobacco, cotton) Americas → Europe.
  • Consumer culture: sugar, coffee, tea and tobacco became everyday purchases for ordinary Europeans, fueling demand and trade.
